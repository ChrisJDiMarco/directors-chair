# THE DIRECTOR’S CHAIR — Complete Film & Television Studio Build Prompt

## Mandate and product promise
Extend the existing Director’s Chair Electron Mac app into a deeply playable filmmaking studio: a place where anyone can be the director, cinematographer, lighting designer, production designer and editor of the same scene. The user learns by making visible choices and seeing their consequences. Preserve working saves, the current diner, the editable lighting rig, camera controls, takes and exports. Inspect the actual repository before changing architecture. This is an extension of a working local app, not permission to replace it with screenshots, a static mockup, a different engine or an unrelated demo.

Use the supplied STORM COLOSSUS: CITYFALL build prompt as a model of specificity, integrated systems and evidence. Translate its discipline into filmmaking; do not inherit its combat, destruction, creature, mobile or provider requirements. Use the existing Director’s Chair concept images as visual direction and compare them with actual app captures. Clearly distinguish a cinematic target image from implemented real-time rendering.

Make strong routine choices independently. Build complete connected experiences, repair integration failures and verify the actual result. Do not describe a control as working because a label changes. Never describe an approximate lighting technique as measured physical simulation. Preserve all provider, license and asset provenance. Use installed tools and approved services; discover exact capabilities before depending on them. Do not invent credentials, available generators, endorsements or professional involvement.

## The fantasy and first minute
The player steps onto a miniature film set, sees a live view through a real virtual camera, and discovers that moving a person, changing a lens or lifting a lamp can change the story. In the first minute they should select a scenario, frame its cast, move a light and record a take. The first creative payoff should happen in seconds, without a tutorial wall or a chat prompt.

Core loop: choose a set and dramatic problem → cast and block the action → establish camera coverage → shape light and production design → record alternate takes → construct a cut → screen and compare → revise one decision → save or export.

Offer three complementary ways to play. Guided scenes give a clear dramatic objective and a small experiment. Free studio gives immediate access to all implemented scene controls. Technical labs isolate variables such as camera distance versus focal length, eye-level versus low angle, or source position versus contrast. These modes share the same renderer, project format and tools.

## Product boundaries and release order
The next executable release is Studio Expansion 0.4: five distinct 3D locations, fifteen authored scenarios, one to eight independently controllable cast slots, named cast, camera targeting across the full cast, three reusable camera banks, scenario-aware teaching prompts, and cross-location takes. Implement these now in the existing app, package them and verify them. This release must be useful on its own.

The later Production release adds timeline-authored performances, continuous camera moves, multicamera synchronized playback, editable sound, color scopes and deeper coaching. These are explicit product requirements for subsequent iterations, not claims about 0.4. Keep a clear implementation-status section in the handoff. Never silently substitute a written roadmap for the executable Studio Expansion release.

Retain a local, offline-capable Electron product. Public deployment, multiplayer, accounts, subscriptions, third-party purchases, celebrity replicas, copyrighted scene reconstruction and a full professional NLE are outside this build. Do not require an AI provider for basic shooting, playback or teaching.

## Five authored production spaces
Each location needs its own geometry, composition, material identity, lighting problem, seating arrangements, useful camera lanes and recognizable props. Changing a location must change the world, not merely the background color or lesson title. Shared materials and modular geometry are encouraged; identical floor plans under different labels are not.

1. Philadelphia diner — The Last Coffee. Preserve walnut paneling, green leather booths, cream tile and dark insets, chrome counter, coffee props, warm pendants and blue-hour windows. Use booth geography to teach eyelines, foreground shoulders, negative space, two-person coverage and continuity. Support leads and background cast while preserving seated contact.
2. Loft apartment — After the Party. An intimate living room with a sectional or sofa, armchairs, textured rug, coffee table, bookshelves, console, art, tall windows and practical lamps. Give the camera foreground layers and enough room for a three- or four-person confrontation. Mix cool window motivation with warm household practicals. Teach triangular staging and revealing information by reframing.
3. Interview room — The Other Side. A restrained institutional room with a central table, two opposing chairs, observation glass, clock, wall panels, evidence folders and a hard overhead source. It should feel deliberately sparse, not unfinished. Teach power, eye level, motivated hardness, clean singles and the difference between secrecy and unreadable underexposure.
4. Rooftop terrace — Ten Minutes Before Dawn. A broad exterior platform with parapets, railings, brick service structure, ducts, seating, planters and a layered city skyline. Use foreground rails and distant scale. Teach ensemble spacing, backlight, skyline separation, silhouettes and establishing shots. Keep the working area legible instead of burying missing geometry in fog.
5. Television studio — Before We’re Live. A physical broadcast set with interview seating, desk, scenic panels, a branded wall, floor marks, practical LED accents and visible production context in Stage mode. Support a host, guests, panelists and background performers. Teach master shot, host single, guest single, reaction and balanced panel coverage. A/B/C banks are stored camera setups in 0.4; do not claim simultaneous live feeds until implemented.

Every location should open with a useful wide frame and a workable lighting preset. Defaults must place feet on the floor, seated bodies on support surfaces and faces within useful camera coverage. Camera framing must account for the complete active cast rather than assuming two people at the diner origin.

## Fifteen directed scenarios
Each card includes set, intended cast count, dramatic objective, teaching concept, starting arrangement, an actionable shot experiment and three coverage suggestions. Loading a scenario changes actual location, cast and starting state. Existing recorded takes remain intact, and Undo restores the previous setup.

Diner / An Unspoken Goodbye / 2 actors. Tell us who is leaving before anyone speaks. Explore negative space and reaction coverage. Make a wide, one restrained single and a held reaction.
Diner / Someone Is Listening / 3 actors. Reveal a third person’s attention without an explanatory cut. Explore foreground/background relationships and an observer’s sightline. Compare a shared frame with isolated coverage.
Diner / The Family Table / 6 actors. Make one quiet person readable in a busy social space. Explore ensemble hierarchy, selective coverage and visual attention. Establish the group before isolating the emotional center.

Loft / The Confession / 2 actors. Make the listener carry the scene. Explore camera height and the timing of a reaction. Compare a balanced two-shot with an unequal composition.
Loft / Three Versions of the Truth / 3 actors. Make changing alliances legible. Explore triangular blocking, screen direction and three-person coverage. Identify whose eyeline organizes each shot.
Loft / The Surprise / 5 actors. Hide a reveal in the room without losing spatial clarity. Explore entrances, foreground masking and a group reaction. The player must be able to show both the reveal and its effect.

Interview / The Last Question / 2 actors. Shift authority without moving the table. Explore hard light, camera height and size in frame. Compare the same face from two contrasting setups.
Interview / The Witness / 3 actors. Distinguish the witness, questioner and observer. Explore asymmetric blocking and reaction ownership. Avoid cutting only to the person who would be speaking.
Interview / Alone With the Evidence / 1 actor. Make an empty chair matter. Explore object relationships, offscreen space and solitude. One-actor mode must work throughout targeting, coverage and saves.

Rooftop / Before Dawn / 2 actors. Make a decision feel larger than the conversation. Explore the skyline, silhouettes and the relation between a wide and a close frame.
Rooftop / The Deal / 4 actors. Clarify who controls the meeting. Explore opposing groups, a shared axis and physical separation. Record a master and coverage of both sides.
Rooftop / The Farewell / 8 actors. Build an ensemble frame with a readable emotional center. Explore depth, overlap and selective isolation. Keep the full cast available to the director and camera targets.

Television / The First Interview / 2 actors. Make host and guest feel connected. Explore a master, host single and guest single using camera banks.
Television / The Panel / 5 actors. Keep a host and four guests visually organized. Explore consistent screen direction, balanced coverage and useful reactions.
Television / Breaking Tonight / 3 actors. Build an urgent but comprehensible broadcast sequence. Explore an anchor, correspondent and analyst through three stored setups and a deliberate cut order.

These are original fictional prompts. Coaching can discuss craft principles; it must not imply that a named professional taught, reviewed or endorsed the app.

## Cast and blocking that remain grounded
Support one to eight active cast slots. Each needs a stable identity, editable name, independent position, yaw, supported seated/standing state and camera/light targeting. Add and remove cast without corrupting earlier takes. Ensure removing a selected cast member leaves a valid selection. A scene with one actor must not reference a missing second actor.

For the first expansion, reuse the two existing licensed rigged actor assets as clearly identified appearance variants. Give each slot an independent skeleton so posing one person cannot deform another. Share geometry and textures where safe. Never claim eight unique scanned people when there are eight slots using two base meshes. Future casting should replace variants with genuinely distinct licensed or generated actors with coherent scale, skin, hair, clothing and close-up quality.

Seated actors must be attached to the correct support transform. Moving or rotating a chair or booth must carry its actor. Fit the pelvis and visible body to the cushion and soles to the intended floor. Do not solve intersection by hiding limbs. Standing controls operate in world space. For occupied seats, reject or explain conflicts rather than stacking people invisibly.

Provide names in the cast list and use those names consistently in camera targets, light targets, shot presets and prompts. Avoid assumptions that actor zero is always the protagonist or that actor one is always present. Show the complete active cast in an establishing setup. Make head and body pose stable across repeated scene switches, undo, take playback and reload.

The Production release should add timed marks, entrances/exits, deliberate head/eyeline targets, listening gestures, sitting/standing transitions and short authored action clips. Author and validate performance in Blender or another explicitly identified workflow; save editable sources. Test deformed meshes and foot contact at multiple times, not just rest-pose screenshots. Do not use random bone shaking as a substitute for acting. Speech, lip sync and emotion controls require integrated, observable behavior before appearing as completed features.

## Cameras, lenses and television coverage
Keep Stage orbit separate from the film camera. Orbiting the editor must not silently rewrite a recorded camera setup. Retain focal length, aperture, focus distance, camera XYZ, target XYZ, exposure, frame guides and double-click focus. Preserve the rendered aspect ratio in the live monitor, saved thumbnail and exported frame, or label any deliberate thumbnail crop.

Support camera banks A, B and C. Saving a bank stores camera position, target, focal length, aperture, focus and exposure. Loading it restores those values without moving actors, changing a set or overwriting lighting. Clearly distinguish Save setup from Load setup. Banks persist in projects and are useful for host/guest/master coverage. Do not show fake live feeds for inactive banks.

Add a selected-cast close setup and complete-cast wide setup. Single-actor, three-person and eight-person cases must all be valid. Compute world-space targets from actual actor and support transforms. Camera and light targeting must follow a chair that has moved or rotated. Do not accidentally target hidden pooled actors.

In the Production release, add start/end camera marks with easing, a timeline playhead, focus pulls, dolly/pan/tilt/crane paths and a collision-aware camera option. Drive motion, playback and export from one deterministic scene clock. A moving take must replay the recorded path, not whichever camera is currently active. Preserve camera state during pause, scrubbing and export cancellation. Add dolly-zoom and perspective labs only when their camera/lens coupling is implemented correctly.

## Lighting and production design
Preserve the full editable rig. Lights have selectable source handles, translation, rotation or aim targets, numeric position, intensity, color, beam spread, enabled state, shadows, solo and reset. Use inverse-square direct light falloff and actual shadow response. Keep source geometry visually aligned with its emitter. A light’s visible housing must not block its own beam accidentally.

Black flags must physically block direct light. Explain the current renderer’s source-softness approximation. Ambient fill is an environment approximation; it is not computed bounce from a white card. Do not present diffusion cloth, reflectors, negative fill or volumetric haze as simulated until a controlled comparison shows the intended behavior. Expose limits where the tool would otherwise teach the wrong lesson.

Retain eight simultaneous controllable sources as a tested initial cap. Budget shadow texture units as well as draw calls; detailed materials plus many shadows can exceed GPU limits. Prefer correct bounded lighting over silent shader failure. Adding a ninth source should explain the cap and preserve the existing rig. Restore original rig is reversible and leaves recorded takes untouched.

Each set uses editable furniture and props with meaningful parent relationships. Use local transforms for cups on tables and world transforms for standing actors. Hide, reset and reposition must affect the actual render and persist. Keep decorative objects outside camera lanes where they are not part of the exercise. Provide clear selection feedback and a way to reset a lost object.

## Material, environment and visual direction
Use the existing references’ miniature film-stage presentation, charcoal interface, amber controls, green upholstery, worn timber, warm practical highlights and cool exterior depth as the starting visual language. Give each new set a related but distinct palette. Television can use cooler scenic accents; the interview room can use desaturated institutional surfaces; the loft can be warmer and layered.

Use real local PBR maps where licensed, correct color-space handling, roughness/normal separation, consistent texture scale, beveled geometry, contact shadows and restrained specular response. Avoid oversized wood grain, shiny plastic skin, razor edges on upholstery, floating props, stretched UVs and repeated decorative patterns that dominate the composition. Maintain highlight detail instead of using bloom to hide missing material work.

Share materials, reuse geometry and instance repeated architecture or furniture when useful. Keep the rich hero diner intact. Do not spend the expansion budget on five shallow copies of the same room. New locations need visible spatial and architectural differences. Show close, wide and overhead views during review, including practical camera angles rather than only flattering dollhouse shots.

Weather belongs to its world. Diner rain should stay outside the windows. Indoor scenes should not inherit visible rain through a ceiling. Exterior skyline and background geometry must switch with the location. Keep source art, experiments and build tooling out of packaged runtime assets when unused.

## Editing, teaching and feedback
Preserve record, thumbnail, take selection, duration, order, playback, deletion, PNG export and silent WebM export. A take stores its own scene ID, scenario ID, cast, lights, object transforms, camera and dressing. A cut can contain takes from different locations. During playback the correct world and cast must return at each cut. Switching scenarios must not discard earlier footage.

Teaching feedback should distinguish observed settings from artistic interpretation. It may report the active cast count, number of camera positions, lens diversity, missing establishing coverage and hold durations. It may suggest experimenting with an angle, a reaction or a light. It must not claim to understand a character’s actual performance from a static pose or assign an objective score to artistic quality.

Scenario-specific prompts should be available where decisions happen, with short text and a concrete next action. Let users try an alternative and compare rather than reading long theory. Free Studio must not lock controls behind lesson completion. Maintain clear state labels for current location, scenario, camera bank and cast size.

The Production release should add A/B take comparison, continuity/axis overlays tied to actual subject positions, editable dialogue/ambience/music lanes, J/L cuts and picture/sound trim. Audio waveforms must come from actual clips. Empty sound lanes must not masquerade as audible performance. Later color tools should include real exposure/white-balance controls and scopes computed from rendered pixels, not decorative graphs.

## Sound and performance roadmap
Use original or licensed sound: room tone appropriate to each location, restrained UI feedback, rain outside the diner, rooftop wind and distant city, household ambience in the loft, and a quiet broadcast floor. Keep master, ambience, dialogue, effects and music levels independent when those systems exist. Unlock audio on a user gesture, deduplicate loops, clean up when switching worlds and preserve pause/resume behavior.

Generated actor dialogue, voice direction or lip sync requires a real integrated provider and explicit asset provenance. Do not imitate a real performer’s voice or claim a named director’s personal coaching. Keep API credentials outside the renderer. When audio is not implemented, label exports as silent. The first expansion remains silent rather than shipping fake sound controls.

## Interface and accessibility
Keep the rendered world dominant. Add a Locations workspace that makes the five sets and their scenarios browseable without hiding the existing camera and light controls. Every scenario card should show cast count and the craft problem. Use a deliberate title hierarchy, concise instructions, selected states and keyboard-accessible buttons. Keep the live camera monitor visible while working on stage.

Blocking gets a real cast roster, Add actor, Remove actor, name editing and appearance selection. Camera gets active-cast targeting and A/B/C setup controls. Lighting and Studio retain direct manipulation and numeric precision. Show capability limits before an action fails. Do not intercept typing or normal text editing with game shortcuts.

Keep the minimum supported Mac window usable at 1120 by 740 logical pixels, with scrollable inspectors and no inaccessible bottom actions. Verify a larger window too. Do not invent mobile support for an Electron-only release. Provide help that describes the controls actually shipped. Preserve focus visibility, reasonable contrast and undo for reversible creative choices.

## Architecture, persistence and performance
Extend the existing Electron/Three.js application. Use simple explicit location and scenario definitions, shared scene-building primitives, stable cast IDs, a bounded actor pool and independent skeleton clones. Reuse the existing scene editor and project serialization. Do not replace the engine or add a second state system merely to imitate the example prompt’s technology list.

Keep context isolation, renderer sandbox, no Node integration, loopback serving, blocked external navigation, minimal validated IPC and packaged local assets. Extend validation for scene IDs, scenario IDs, one-to-eight actors, appearance choices, camera-bank vectors and all new bounded arrays. Read legacy projects without deleting or silently rewriting the originals. Reject corrupt imports before changing current state.

A scenario transition must change set visibility, cast, seating, environment and lights coherently. Hidden locations must not remain as invisible occluders or emitters. Inactive actor slots must not appear in the render, targets or exports. Shared geometry is safe; shared skeleton pose state is not. Repeated location changes must not accumulate lights, textures, meshes, event handlers or audio sources.

Start with a 60 FPS desktop target, then report measured behavior separately. Use a DPR cap, existing antialiasing/post effects and bounded shadows. Count full-frame work including the preview monitor and shadow passes; requestAnimationFrame frequency alone is not a GPU performance measurement. Keep a stable lower-quality option as a future requirement if eight actors or the new locations exceed actual device budgets. Do not hide severe performance failures behind an unmeasured target.

## Studio Expansion acceptance checks
The release is ready only when the following are demonstrated in the built app.

Location breadth: five visibly distinct 3D sets are selectable; all fifteen scenario definitions load their intended set and cast. Capture each set from a useful stage view and the film camera. Indoor scenes do not inherit exterior rain incorrectly.
Cast: exercise one, two, three, five and eight actors; add and remove at both limits; rename a cast member; change appearance; pose and move one actor without deforming another. Seated actors stay attached to the intended support. Hidden pool slots stay hidden.
Cameras: frame all eight actors, target any named cast member, save and recall A/B/C without changing scene content, and preserve focus/optics through project reload.
Scene continuity: record takes in at least two different locations, play them in sequence and verify that cast, set, props and lighting restore at each cut. Earlier diner takes remain playable.
Lighting regression: source translation/aim/intensity changes the rendered image; a flag blocks direct illumination; the cap prevents shader resource failure; duplicated and removed lights remain consistent after reload.
Persistence: save/open, autosave recovery, undo/redo, legacy two-person projects, new cast/location state, corrupt-import rejection and canceled dialogs remain safe.
Output: PNG and WebM exports contain actual rendered content with the expected aspect and scene. Silent output is identified accurately. No generated screenshot is substituted for footage.
Visual/runtime: no fatal JavaScript, asset-loading or WebGL error in tested states; inspect seating and material scale, object overlap, camera framing, clipped UI, hidden-world leakage and accumulated resources. Preserve exact reproducible checks and resulting images.
Packaging: produce an Apple-silicon .app, ad-hoc sign it, verify the signature and test the packaged runtime. Keep the previous release available. Report the exact new artifact and distinguish isolated QA from an owner-visible native-window check. If native inspection is blocked, state that specific remaining check without claiming completion.
